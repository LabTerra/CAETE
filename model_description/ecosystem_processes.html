Ecosystem Processes

For the next sections, the symbols i, y, and z refer to a PLS, a grid cell, and a plant compartment, respectively.

Productivity

The ecophysiological processes that determine potential primary productivity are calculated for each PLS within a grid cell. Cell-level plant biomass stocks and biogeochemical fluxes are obtained as abundance-weighted community-weighted means (CWMs) of all PLS present.

CAETÊ implements both C3 (Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry) and C4 (Chen et al., 1994) photosynthetic pathways. The maximum carboxylation rate of Rubisco depends on leaf nitrogen content (Domingues et al., 2010). These biochemical models respond to mesophyll temperature, atmospheric CO2 concentration, and photosynthetically active radiation.

Photosynthesis

The photosynthesis equation (\(GPP_{i,y}\)) and the ones associated with it are based on Farquhar, Caemmerer, & Berry (1980) formulation, which takes into account three limiting factors: Rubisco carboxylation (\(J_{c}\)), light (\(J_{L_i}\)), and electron transport (\(J_{E}\)). We also included water stress limitation (\(f_{5,i}\)).

(1)\[GPP_{i,y} = 0.012 \times 31\,557\,600 \times f_{1,i,y}^{\,4} \times f_{4,i,y}^{\,\text{sun}} \times f_{4,i,y}^{\,\text{shade}} \times f_{5,i}\]

where:

  • \(f_{1,i,y}\) is the leaf level gross photosynthesis;

  • \(f_{4,i,y}^{\text{sun}}\) and \(f_{4,i,y}^{\text{shade}}\) are functions for upscaling the leaf level photosynthesis to the canopy level;

  • \(f_{5,i}\) is the water stress limitation.

In the following equations, \(k_n\) are constants summarized in (Definitions, units, and equation tags for all variables described in CAETÊ (Eqs. 1-63)). The factor \(f_{1i,y}\) is calculated as the smallest root among the three limiting rates: \(J_{C\,i,y}\), \(J_{L\,i,y}\) and \(J_E\):

(2)\[f_{1i,y} \;=\; \text{smallest root of}\;\bigl\{\,k_{1}J^{2}\;-\;J\,(J_{P\,i,y}+J_{E})\;+\;J_{P\,i,y}\,J_{E}\;=\;0\bigr\}\]

where \(J_{P\,i,y}\) is itself the minimum between \(J_{C\,i,y}\) and \(J_{L\,i,y}\), defined by:

(3)\[J_{P\,i,y} \;=\; \text{smallest root of}\;\bigl\{\,k_{2}J_{P\,i,y}^{2}\;-\;J_{P\,i,y}\,(J_{C\,i,y}+J_{L\,i,y})\;+\;J_{C\,i,y}J_{L\,i,y}\;=\;0\bigr\}\]

The Rubisco‑limited carboxylation rate \(J_{C,i,y}\) depends on several factors:

  • the maximum carboxylation capacity \(V_{m,i,y}\),

  • the internal CO2 partial pressure \(C_{\text{press},i,y}\),

  • the photorespiratory compensation point \(\Gamma_{i,y}\),

  • and the Michaelis–Menten constants \(f_{2i,y}\) for CO2 and \(f_{3i,y}\) for O2.

And is given by:

(4)\[J_{C\,i,y} = V_{m,i,y}\, \left\{\,C_{\text{press},i,y} -\frac{\Gamma_{i,y}}{C_{\text{press},i,y}} +f_{2i,y}\right\} \Biggl[\,1 + \Bigl(\tfrac{k_{3}}{f_{3i,y}}\Bigr)\Biggr]\]

The temperature dependencies to \(f_{2i,y}\) and \(f_{3i,y}\):

(5)\[f_{2i,y} = k_{12}\,k_{13}^{10\,(T_{y}-k_{11})}\]
(6)\[f_{3i,y} = k_{14}\,k_{15}^{10\,(T_{y}-k_{11})}\]

The internal CO2 pressure is adjusted for leaf water deficit \(r_{i,y}\) as:

(7)\[C_{\text{press},i,y} = k_{16}\,\Bigl[\,1 - \bigl(\tfrac{r_{i,y}}{k_{17}}\bigr)\Bigr]\,(CO_{2y}-\Gamma_{i,y}) + \Gamma_{i,y}\]

The compensation point \(\Gamma_{i,y}\) also depends on temperature:

(8)\[\Gamma_{i,y} = \Bigl(\tfrac{k_{3}}{k_{8}}\Bigr)\;k_{9}^{10\,(T_{y}-k_{11})}\]

Water stress modifies the effective CO2 pressure via the leaf‑level moisture deficit \(r_{i,y}\), while \(C_{a,y}\) denotes the ambient CO2 concentration (input). The deficit is calculated as a fixed proportion of the saturation mixing ratio \(r_{\max,i,y}\):

(9)\[r_{i,y} = -0.315\,r_{\max,i,y}\]

Here, \(r_{\max,i,y}\) is itself a function of the leaf vapor pressure \(w_{\text{press},i,y}\) and the surface pressure \(P_{\text{surf},y}\).

Finally, \(r_{\max,i,y}\) is a function of the partial pressure of water vapor \(w_{\text{press},i,y}\) and the surface pressure \(P_{\text{surf},y}\):

(10)\[r_{\max,i,y} = 0.622\,\frac{w_{\text{press},i,y}}{P_{\text{surf},y}-w_{\text{press},i,y}}\]

The leaf interior vapor pressure \(w_{\text{press},i,y}\) is calculated as a function of temperature \(T_y\):

(11)\[w_{\text{press},i,y} \;=\; 6.1121 \times \exp\!\Biggl\{\Bigl[\,18.678 \;-\;\frac{T_y}{234.5}\Bigr]\times\Bigl[\;\tfrac{T_y}{257.14 + T_y}\Bigr]\Biggr\}\]

The light‑limited photosynthetic rate \(J_{L,i,y}\) depends on the incident photosynthetically active radiation (IPARi,y); here IPAR is taken as 50% of the incoming shortwave radiation (input):

(12)\[J_{L,i,y} \;=\; k_{4}\,(1 - k_{5})\,\mathrm{IPAR}_{i,y}\]

The electron‑transport‑limited rate \(J_{E,i,y}\) is

(13)\[J_{E,i,y} \;=\; k_{7}\,V_{m,i,y}\]

where the Rubisco maximum rate \(V_{m,i,y}\) itself is temperature‑dependent:

(14)\[V_{m,i,y} = V_{c\max,i,y}\,\frac{k_{10}^{(T_y - k_{11})}}{k_{18}} \;+\; \exp\!\bigl(k_{19}\,(T_y - k_{20})\bigr)\]

and \(V_{c\max,i,y}\) (Definitions, units, and equation tags for all variables described in CAETÊ (Eqs. 1-63)) is the maximum Rubisco carboxylation rate, with \(T_y\) as input temperature.

The canopy‑scaling factor \(f_{4,i,y}\) is split into sunlit and shaded components, \(f_{4,i,y}^{\mathrm{sun}}\) and \(f_{4,i,y}^{\mathrm{shade}}\). The sunlit portion assumes direct radiation at a 90° incidence, while the shaded portion receives diffuse light at 20°:

(15)\[f_{4,i,y}^{\mathrm{sun}} = \frac{1 - \exp\bigl(-k_{21}\,\mathrm{LAI}_{\mathrm{sun},i,y}\bigr)}{k_{21}}\]
(16)\[f_{4,i,y}^{\mathrm{shade}} = \frac{1 - \exp\bigl(-k_{22}\,\mathrm{LAI}_{\mathrm{shade},i,y}\bigr)}{k_{22}}\]

Here \(\mathrm{LAI}_{i,y}\) is partitioned into \(\mathrm{LAI}_{\mathrm{sun},i,y}\) and \(\mathrm{LAI}_{\mathrm{shade},i,y}\) according to canopy geometry. Total LAI is

(17)\[\mathrm{LAI}_{i,y} = C_{\mathrm{leaf},i,y}\,\mathrm{SLA}_{i}\]

where \(C_{\mathrm{leaf},i,y}\) is leaf carbon per unit ground area and \(\mathrm{SLA}_{i,y}\) is the specific leaf area (a variant functional trait in this version).

Light availability and light competition

In CAETÊ, light competition is represented by a straightforward vertical-stratification scheme in which each PLS captures a different share of incident light. Each PLS within a grid cell is assigned to a vertical layer according to its height. This layer position determines the light available to the PLS, since canopy light attenuation obeys the Lambert–Beer law. Consequently, radiation‑dependent processes, such as photosynthesis and even allometric relationships, are modulated by the local light environment.

The Lambert–Beer law describes how the intensity of a light beam decreases exponentially as it travels through an absorbing medium (Oldham & Parnis, 2017) and is commonly applied in ecology to model light attenuation within forest canopies (Hirose, 2005).

To stratify PLS into discrete vertical layers, the model defines:

  1. N_layer: the total number of layers, computed from the maximum height of each PLS (H_max, in meters);

  2. Size_layer: the uniform length of each layer (in meters) across the entire canopy profile.

(18)\[\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{layer}} \;=\;\frac{\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{max}}}{5}\]

where 5 is a standardized value for all grid cells, used to set the vertical stratification.

(19)\[\mathrm{Size}_{\mathrm{layer}} \;=\;\frac{\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{max}}}{\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{layer}}}\]

The incident light (photosynthetically active radiation, IPAR; denoted \(\mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{inc}}\)) in each layer depends on the light arriving in the previous layer (\(\mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{aval},\,l-1}\)) minus the light consumed by the PLSs in that layer (\(\mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{used},\,l}\)):

(20)\[\mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{inc},\,l} \;=\; \mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{aval},\,l-1} \;-\; \mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{used},\,l}\]

In the topmost layer, \(\mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{inc}}\) is simply the absolute IPAR (in \(\mathrm{J}\,\mathrm{m}^{-2}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\)).

\(\mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{used}}\) is calculated using an extinction coefficient of 0.59, typical for broadleaf forests (Binkley et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2014), following the Lambert–Beer law (Sitch et al., 2003; Hirose, 2005):

(21)\[\mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{used},\,l} \;=\; \mathrm{Light}_{\mathrm{aval},\,l} \bigl[\,1 - \exp\bigl(-0.59\,\mathrm{LAI}_{\mathrm{mean\ layer}}\bigr)\bigr]\]

The amount of light received by each PLS is fed into the photosynthesis functions (\(J_{L,i,y}\)) mentioned above. All PLS within the same layer receive the same amount of available light.

Respiration

Autotrophic respiration, \(R_{a,i,y}\), is partitioned into growth respiration \(R_{g,i,y}\) and maintenance respiration \(R_{m,i,y}\) according to Ryan, 1991a,b.

(22)\[R_{a,i,y} \;=\; R_{g,i,y} \;+\; R_{m,i,y}\]

Growth Respiration

We assume that the metabolic cost of constructing new tissue consumes 25% of the carbon allocated. Denoting the carbon in compartment \(z\) at time \(t\) by \(C_{z,i,y}^t\), the total growth respiration across the three structural pools (leaves, fine roots, woody tissues) is:

(23)\[R_{g,i,y} \;=\; \sum_{z=1}^{3} 0.25\,\bigl(C_{z,i,y}^t \;-\; C_{z,i,y}^{t-1}\bigr)\]

Maintenance Respiration

Maintenance respiration is calculated from the nitrogen and carbon content of each tissue. Although roughly 60% of this respiration supports protein maintenance and replacement, the model imposes nitrogen limitation on maintenance costs according to C and N pools.

(24)\[R_{m,i,y} \;=\; \sum_{z=1}^{3} \bigl[n_{c,z}\,C_{z,i,y}^{t}\,\exp\bigl(0.07\,T_{y}\bigr)\bigr]\]

Here:

  • \(n_{c,z}\) is the N:C ratio of compartment \(z\) (leaves, sapwood, fine roots).

  • \(C_{z,i,y}^{t}\) is the carbon content of compartment \(z\) at time \(t\).

  • \(T_{y}\) is the mean annual temperature (°C).

Because heartwood is largely non‑respiring, only 5% of aboveground woody carbon (sapwood) contributes to maintenance respiration, representing the metabolically active fraction (Pavlick et al., 2003). Typical N:C ratios used are 0.034 (leaves), 0.003 (sapwood), and 0.034 (fine roots, Levis et al., 2004; Sitch et al., 2003). In tropical forests, fine‑root respiration is driven by soil temperature \(T_{\mathrm{soil},y}\) rather than air temperature (Oyama and Nobre, 2004).

Stomatal Conductance and Canopy Resistance

Stomatal conductance \(g_{s,i,y}\) and canopy resistance \(C_{r,i,y}\) couple the carbon assimilation (\(GPP_{i,y}\)) to the water‑balance submodel:

(25)\[C_{r,i,y} \;=\; \frac{1}{g_{s,i,y}}\]
(26)\[g_{s,i,y} \;=\; g_{0} \;+\; 1.6\, \Bigl(1 + \tfrac{g_{1}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{VPD}_{y}}}\Bigr)\, \frac{GPP_{i,y}}{C_{a,y}}\]

where:

  • \(g_{0}=0.001\) mol m-2 s-1 is the residual conductance,

  • \(g_{1}\) is the sensitivity to carbon assimilation (Medlyn et al.,2011) and a variant functional trait (Descriptions, values, and units of the constant parameters k1-k22 and allometric constants in CAETÊ.),

  • \(\mathrm{VPD}_{y}\) is the vapor‑pressure deficit at the leaf surface.

The leaf‑surface vapor‑pressure deficit is computed as:

(27)\[\mathrm{VPD}_{y} \;=\; \frac{E_{\mathrm{vap},i,y}\,h_{y}}{10}\]

where \(h_{y}\) is the ambient relative humidity and \(E_{\mathrm{vap},i,y}\) is the transpiration flux.

Carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus allocation

In CAETÊ the process of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus allocation follows 8 main daily steps:

1. Initial NPP partitioning

Daily net primary production (NPP; \(\mathrm{g\,C\,m^{-2}\,day^{-1}}\)) is divided among leaves (\(\ell\)), wood (\(w\)), and fine roots (\(r\)) according to fixed allocation fractions \(\alpha_z\) (\(\alpha_\ell+\alpha_w+\alpha_r=1\)).

The potential carbon input for compartment \(z\) is

(28)\[C_z^{\mathrm{pot}} = \alpha_z \,\mathrm{NPP}.\]

2. Stoichiometric nutrient demand

For each compartment \(z\), the required nitrogen and phosphorus are calculated as

(29)\[N_z^{\mathrm{demand}} = \rho_i^{N\!:\!C}\,C_i^{\mathrm{pot}}, \qquad P_z^{\mathrm{demand}} = \rho_z^{P\!:\!C}\,C_i^{\mathrm{pot}},\]

where \(\rho_i^{N\!:\!C}\) and \(\rho_z^{P\!:\!C}\) are PLS‑specific mass ratios.

3. Daily nutrient availability

Define internal reserves \(S_{N},S_{P}\), passive uptake \(U_{N}^{\mathrm{pas}},U_{P}^{\mathrm{pas}}\), active uptake \(U_{N}^{\mathrm{act}},U_{P}^{\mathrm{act}}\), and biological N fixation \(F_{N}\). Then

(30)\[N_{\mathrm{disp}} = S_{N} + U_{N}^{\mathrm{pas}} + U_{N}^{\mathrm{act}} + F_{N},\]
(31)\[P_{\mathrm{disp}} = S_{P} + U_{P}^{\mathrm{pas}} + U_{P}^{\mathrm{act}}\]

4. N‑ and P‑Limited Carbon Allocation

The real carbon allocated to compartment \(z\) is the minimum of its potential and nutrient‑limited values:

(32)\[C_{z}^{\mathrm{real}} =\min\!\Bigl( C_{z}^{\mathrm{pot}},\, \frac{N_{\mathrm{disp}}\,\alpha_{z}}{\rho_{z}^{N:C}},\, \frac{P_{\mathrm{disp}}\,\alpha_{z}}{\rho_{z}^{P:C}} \Bigr)\]

Total allocated carbon is

(33)\[C^{\mathrm{alloc}} =\sum_{z\in\{\ell,w,r\}}C_{z}^{\mathrm{real}}\]

5. Carbon reserve dynamics and growth respiration

Any unallocated NPP, is stored in a non‑structural carbon pool and incurs growth respiration equal to 25% of the carbon added during the time step.

(34)\[\Delta S_{C} =\mathrm{NPP} \;-\; C^{\mathrm{alloc}}\]

This reserve incurs growth respiration at 25% of the influx:

(35)\[R_{\mathrm{growth}}^{\mathrm{reserve}} =0.25\,\Delta S_{C}\]

6. Updating N and P reserves

Surplus or deficit nutrients after biomass construction are added to reserves: \(\Delta S_N\) and \(\Delta S_P\), which may be positive (store) or negative (drawdown)

(36)\[\Delta S_{N} = N_{\mathrm{disp}} - \sum_{z}\rho_{z}^{N:C}\,C_{z}^{\mathrm{real}},\]
(37)\[\Delta S_{P} = P_{\mathrm{disp}} - \sum_{z}\rho_{z}^{P:C}\,C_{z}^{\mathrm{real}}\]

7. Senescence, litter production, and resorption

Leaf turnover exports litter carbon \(L_C^\ell\) and returns a fraction of its N and P to the plant via resorption;

(38)\[\begin{split}L_{C}^{\ell} = t_{\ell}\,M_{\ell},\\ R_{N}^{\ell} = f_{\mathrm{res}}\,\bigl(\rho_{\ell}^{N:C}\,L_{C}^{\ell}\bigr),\end{split}\]

Where: \(M_{\ell}\) is leaf biomass, \(t_{\ell}\) is turnover rate, and \(f_{\mathrm{res}}\) is resorption fraction.

The remainder enters the soil as organic matter:

(39)\[(1 - f_{\mathrm{res}})\,\rho_{\ell}^{N:C}\,L_{C}^{\ell}\]

8. Energetic cost of nutrient acquisition

Carbon costs for active uptake and resorption are computed by functions \(g_N, g_P\) (active transport) and \(h_N, h_P\) (resorption).

(40)\[C_{\mathrm{cost}}^{N} = g_{N}\bigl(U_{N}^{\mathrm{act}}\bigr) + h_{N}\bigl(R_{N}^{\ell}\bigr)\]
(41)\[C_{\mathrm{cost}}^{P} = g_{P}\bigl(U_{P}^{\mathrm{act}}\bigr) + h_{P}\bigl(R_{P}^{\ell}\bigr)\]

The total cost is subtracted from the plant’s carbon budget.

(42)\[C_{\mathrm{cost}}^{\mathrm{total}} = C_{\mathrm{cost}}^{N} + C_{\mathrm{cost}}^{P}\]

Allometry and tree architecture

Allometry in CAETÊ addresses both how carbon is allocated among leaves, fine roots, and non‑photosynthetic tissues (handled in the carbon‑allocation and residence‑time module; Rius et al. 2023) and the plant’s architectural growth. This approach is based on LPJ model (Sitch et al. 2003)

To PLS height \(H\) (in meters):

(43)\[H \;=\; K_{\mathrm{alom2}}\;\;\mathrm{Diam}^{\mathrm{K_alom3}}\]

where \(K_{\mathrm{alom2}}\) and \(K_{\mathrm{alom3}}\) are allometric constants fixed (Definitions, units, and equation tags for all variables described in CAETÊ (Eqs. 1-63)), and \(\mathrm{Diam}\) is the stem diameter in centimeters.

The crown area \(Ca\) (in m2) is given by:

(44)\[Ca \;=\; K_{\mathrm{alom1}}\;\mathrm{Diam}^{k_{\mathrm{rp}}}\]

where \(K_{\mathrm{alom1}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{rp}}\) are allometric constants fixed (Definitions, units, and equation tags for all variables described in CAETÊ (Eqs. 1-63)), and \(\mathrm{Diam}\) again is the stem diameter in centimeters.

Finally, the stem diameter itself (\(\mathrm{Diam}\), in cm) is determined from the stem carbon stock \(C_{\mathrm{stem}}\) and wood density (\(\mathrm{WD}\)):

(45)\[\mathrm{Diam} \;=\; \frac{4\,C_{\mathrm{wood}}} {\bigl(\mathrm{WD}\ \pi\ 40\bigr)^{\tfrac{1}{2}+0.5}}\]
